# Offers lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **The pain is massive.** A rider lying unconscious while family and helpers don't know where he is or what his blood group is: the dream outcome ("help reaches you fast, the next rider is warned") is about as strong as an outcome gets.
- **Low effort for the rider, if it works.** It runs in the background and needs no reporting. That is the best possible score on effort and sacrifice.
- **There is a stack to build.** Crash SOS, medical ID, hospital routing, hazard warnings and red zones are separate solutions to separate problems on the way to one outcome. Each can be valued and bundled, which is the opposite of a commodity.
- **One candidate buyer is concentrated.** Delivery riders are reached through a small number of fleets and gig platforms. Selling to one platform could reach thousands of riders at once.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **The person with the pain is not the person with the money.** The pain belongs to the rider, but the brief gives no evidence that riders will pay. Of the four candidate models, rider freemium (a) is the weakest market under this lens. City bodies (d) have money but are slow and hard to reach. If no buyer with a budget feels this pain, the offer fails however good it is.
2. **No proof, so likelihood is zero.** There is no accuracy data, no false-alarm rate, no pilot. A fleet or insurer cannot believe the claim, and a false SOS to a rider's family costs trust as fast as a missed crash does. Most offers fail here, and this one has not started on it.
3. **Value arrives rarely.** A crash alert pays off only when a crash happens. Hazard warnings come more often, but they only work once the network is dense. Until then the buyer pays every month for something they rarely see, plus the battery drain (not addressed in the brief). That is a slow time-to-value problem.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Which buyer already pays today for rider safety, accidents or insurance claims, and how much? (Open. Answer with /founder-consumer for buyer interviews and /founder-competitors for what they buy now.)
- What does one rider crash cost a fleet in downtime, liability, insurance or churn? That number sets the price ceiling. (Open: /founder-cfo.)
- What are your false-positive and false-negative rates on real two-wheeler rides?
- What would you guarantee a fleet, for example a measured drop in response time or in incidents on flagged roads?
- Could you double the per-rider price and still be the obvious choice over doing nothing?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
**The model I would back: (b) a B2B per-rider fee to delivery fleets and gig platforms, with (c) insurers added as the second buyer once there is data.** The fleet has the money, is easy to find and owns the rider relationship. The insurer can later pay for the risk data and the crash alerts the fleet's riders generate. Keep the rider app free, because free riders build the hazard map that makes the offer worth more to both. Treat (d) cities as a later bonus, not a revenue plan.

Fund if, before the raise:
1. One fleet or platform signs a paid pilot or a letter of intent at a stated per-rider price (price still open: /founder-pricing, /founder-cfo).
2. A field test reports crash-detection and false-alarm rates on real rides.
3. The offer is written as a stack with a named guarantee tied to a fleet outcome, not "an app."
4. The cost of a crash to a fleet is measured, so the price is set by value, not by being cheap.
