# Monopoly lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **RoadMemory could compound.** Each rider who hits a pothole makes the map better for the next rider. That is the only thing in the brief that could become a network effect. Crash SOS on its own does not compound.
- **A small market it could own exists.** Delivery riders in one metro are dense, ride all day on the same roads, and are organised under a few fleets. Reaching enough of them to make the hazard map useful is a realistic first goal.
- **Model (b), a per-rider fee paid by fleets, gives it distribution.** One fleet deal brings many riders, and the riders install the app as part of their job, not by choosing it in an app store. That beats chasing riders one by one.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **The buyer could build it.** The gig platforms that would pay for model (b) already have the riders, the app on their phones and the GPS traces. If the idea works, they could copy it in month six. Nothing in the brief stops that: no proprietary technology, no data rights, no locked-in contracts.
2. **Nothing shows the technology is ten times better.** The deck's own table names phone crash detection, maps apps and pothole apps as alternatives. There is no accuracy data and no prototype metrics, and nothing on battery drain or OS limits on background apps. If detection is merely as good as what phones already do, this is an incremental improvement in a crowded field.
3. **The network has a cold start.** Warnings only help once several riders have confirmed a hazard. Below some unknown rider density in one city, RoadMemory is empty and the product shrinks to a crash alert. Model (a), a rider freemium subscription, makes this worse: it starts from zero riders and has no distribution.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- What is the secret? What do you know about how two-wheelers crash or skid that the phone makers and the platforms do not?
- Why now? Why couldn't this have been built five years ago?
- How many active riders per square km does a warning need before it is reliable? (/founder-cfo can model this, from pilot data you do not have yet.)
- What do the first 100 fleet riders cost to acquire, and who signs the deal? (/founder-consumer, /founder-cfo)
- Who already sells crash or safety features to these fleets, and do the platforms have an in-house roadmap for this? (/founder-competitors)
- If a platform walks away, who owns the hazard map?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
**The model I would back:** (b) first, then (d) and (c) as expansion. Use fleet per-rider fees to reach density in one metro. Then sell the anonymised hazard map to the city (d) and risk data to insurers (c), from a data asset no single fleet holds. Drop (a) as the lead model: it has no distribution and must compete with features riders already get free.

1. Pick one metro and sign one fleet pilot or letter of intent. A contract with a fleet is the first proof of distribution.
2. Every fleet contract must give the startup the rights to the anonymised hazard data and the right to pool it across fleets. Otherwise the moat belongs to the customer.
3. Show measured crash and pothole detection accuracy, plus battery cost, from real rides, compared against built-in phone crash detection.
4. Set a density target for the pilot city and show the hazard map getting more useful as riders are added. That proves the network effect exists, not just the claim.
